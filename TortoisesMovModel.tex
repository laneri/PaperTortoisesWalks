\section*{Introduction}
Understanding the mechanisms underlying animal trajectories is crucial for predicting animal behavior, managing wildlife populations, and conserving biodiversity. They are the result of a complex interplay between internal and external factors, including the animal's physiological state, environmental conditions, and interactions with other individuals.

Empirical evidence of Lévy-walks has been reported, among others, for micro-organisms, bumblebees, spider monkeys, marine predators and humans \cite{boyer2009}. Human individuals also display multiple scale displacements well described by truncated power-laws \cite{gonzalez2009}. It has been proposed that the origin of those power law step size distribution emerge after performing a food search following a cognitive map, with some spots visited with a weight that depends on the distance to the spot and the amount of food available \cite{boyer2009}. For instance, previous studies of deer foraging in non patchy habitats found a powerlaw like distribution of step sizes that were succesfully modeled as a combination of a random search for food, coupled with a threshold in the selection of foraging stations \cite{focardi1996}. In general this hypothesis relies on the idea that the distribution of time intervals characterizing the dynamics of many behaviors is scale invariant, with negative exponents ranging from 0,97--2,7 \cite{proekt2012}.

Other studies have suggested that the observed scaling laws in animal movement patterns may arise from the animal's foraging strategy, where they optimize their search for resources by adopting a Lévy walk pattern. Those studies have reported an slope of $\approx -2$ for the step size distribution \cite{viswanathan1999}.  

For the case of reptiles, like tortoises, waiting times are very related to the need to sunbasking as this ectothermic animals depend on sun radiation to thermoregulate their body temperature. The waiting times are also related to the need to rest and digest food, as well as to avoid predators \cite{}. The step size distribution is related to the need to find food, water, and mates, as well as to avoid predators and other threats \cite{}. The movement patterns of tortoises are also influenced by their social behavior, as they may move in groups or follow the movements of other individuals \cite{madile}.

\section*{Methodology}
The study was carried out in a site of approximately 25 hectares, located about 20 km north of the city of San Antonio Oeste, Province of Río Negro, Argentina, where there is a stable population of C. chilensis with a large number of specimens. The area in general belongs to the  Monte Austral phytogeographic unit, characterized by a shrub steppe with a predominance of  Larrea spp. with several strata, the lower one being grasses and herbs, all with very little coverage, and particularly with very few cacti (Oyarzabal et al. 2018).
The specific study area is characterized by a vegetation cover, mostly with xerophytic characteristics, with a predominance of grass clumps (Poaceae) and shrubs (León et al. 1998, Morello et al. 2012). In turn, the grass stratum has a higher species richness than the other Zygophyllaceae of the Monte steppes. The soil is rather sandy with a low percentage of clay and abundant pebbles. Close to the border of the study area, there is the Gran Bajo del Gualicho, a Patagonian plain of up to 70 meters below sea level, characterized by a high saline concentration. This is why there are more saline sectors, where we can find halophyte species such as Suaeda divaricata and several species of the genus Atriplex spp. Scarce precipitations give an average of approximately 255 mm in the year, with minor peaks in spring and fall. Annual average temperature is around 14.5 °C with significant daily thermal amplitudes and a persistence of the west and southwest winds, making the region more arid (Godagnone and Bran, 2008). The minimum average temperature is 1°C, and the maximum is 30 °C, with extreme recorded temperatures of -11.5 and 44.6 °C (data for 1961--2021 provided by the National Meteorological Service of Argentina).
We tracked six tortoises continuously during a whole year using GPS data loggers (i-gotU GT-120 Mobile Action, Taiwan), recording geographical coordinates every 15 minutes. The positions were recorded continuously from 6am to 10pm and from 2am to 3am. The equipment was attached to the carapace of each individual using a camouflage tape (@Duck Tape, Real Tree Hardwood Camouflage) to mimic the environment, with the precaution of not leaving any sharp corners, in order to prevent the tortoise from getting trapped in the vegetation. After attaching the device, tortoises were released at the same place that were found. The weight of the equipment never exceeded 10% of the body mass, in order not to disturb tortoises' activities. 
In this work we present analysis and results spanning a whole year, involving a total of 20149 hours of GPS data loggers recording. Those recorded geographical positions corresponded to 813 trajectories from 6 individuals. The GPS data loggers were charged every fifteen days except during the brumation period, when animals were not disturbed and were observed every two weeks to check if they remained brumating at the same location. All recorded trajectories were preprocessed to exclude those corresponding to speeds above a threshold of 15 m/min, assessed as a reasonable maximum speed for the species, according to the speed empirical distribution presented in Fig.S3. The distance traveled as a function of time was calculated from the $(\mathrm{latitude}, \mathrm{longitude})$ data using the Haversine function.
For each behavioral period, we calculated the mean square displacement (MSD) as a function of time, defined as explained in the supplement and was averaged over all daily mesasurements.
Additionally, accelerometer signal was classified into two states: walking and resting, using the random forest classification algorithm in Python and we calculated the distribution of step lengths and waiting times for each state. The distributions were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation, for each individual and behavioral period. The results were analyzed to identify patterns and differences between individuals, sexes, and behavioral periods.

To bridge the two time resolutions (1.7~s for the accelerometer and 15~min for the GPS), we proposed a stochastic Stop-and-Go model in which each simulation step corresponds to one
accelerometer window (1.7~s). Each individual can be in one of two states, walking or resting. While resting, it neither moves nor rotates, so it keeps its heading. While walking,
at every step it updates its heading as $\theta_{i+1} = \theta_i + \eta_i$, where $\eta_i$ is drawn from a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and standard deviation $\sigma_\theta$, a
parameter of the model, and then moves a constant step of length $L = 1$ (arbitrary units) in the new direction. The time spent in each state is either constant or drawn from a
truncated power-law distribution, $f(t) \propto t^{-\alpha}$ for $t_{\min} \le t \le t_{\max}$, with exponents $\alpha_W$ for walking and $\alpha_S$ for
resting, which are parameters of the simulation. Finally, to compare with the distance travelled between consecutive GPS fixes, we recorded the net displacement every 530
simulation steps (901~s $\approx$ 15~min).

\section*{Modeling Tortoise Movement Patterns}
\label{P:Modelo}
Inspired in the previous field measurements we proposed a model based on a \textit{Stop and Go} random walker moving in a homogeneous continuous space. We simulate $N\approx 100.000$ individuals with no interaction between them, each alternating between two states: movement (\textit{Go}) and rest (\textit{Stop}). 

We assume that each tortoise performs random walks with constant speed interspersed with resting periods, provided the duration of the walking bouts is distributed with a power-law, or Pareto, distribution,
$$  P_b(t) = \frac{\alpha-1}{t^\alpha}, \qquad t\ge1, $$
where \(P_b(t)\,dt\) is the probability that a bout of walking will last from time \(t=0\) to a time between \(t\) and \(t+dt\).

At each step, we draw the total time to be spent in a state directly upon entering it
from its full duration distribution. To this end, we use inverse transform sampling (\textit{inverse-CDF sampling}): given a probability 
density $f(t)\propto t^{-\alpha}$ truncated between a minimum time $t_{min}$ and a maximum time $t_{max}$, its cumulative distribution 
function can be inverted to obtain a closed-form expression that, given a random number $u$ uniformly distributed in $[0,1]$, 
directly yields the sampled time:

\begin{equation}
    t = \left[(t_{max}^{\,1-\alpha} - t_{min}^{\,1-\alpha})\,u + t_{min}^{\,1-\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
\end{equation}

This method yields, in a single operation, the full time that an individual will spend in the movement or rest state, without having to
evaluate the transition probability step by step. Since we simulate a large number of individuals over long time spans, we reduce computational cost.
The individual moves at discrete time intervals $\delta t$, taking steps of constant length $L$. The direction of
each step follows a correlated random walk: the new orientation angle $\theta_{i+1}$ is obtained from
the angle of the previous step, $\theta_i$, by adding Gaussian noise with zero mean and standard deviation $\sigma_\theta$,

\begin{equation}
    \theta_{i+1} = \theta_i + \eta_i, \qquad \eta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_\theta^2).
\end{equation}

The position of the individual is therefore updated at each time step according to

\begin{equation}
    x_{i+1} = x_i + L\cos(\theta_{i+1}), \qquad y_{i+1} = y_i + L\sin(\theta_{i+1}).
\end{equation}

This angular correlation introduces directional persistence into the trajectory: unlike a simple random walk, in which the direction is drawn
independently at each step, the individual tends to maintain its heading over short time scales, producing locally straight trajectories that
curve gradually as angular perturbations accumulate.

In the \textit{Stop} state, by contrast, the position of the individual remains fixed, $x_{i+1}=x_i$ and $y_{i+1}=y_i$, and the only process
that advances is the count of the time elapsed in that state.

The duration of each bout, both \textit{Go} and \textit{Stop}, is drawn upon entering the corresponding state using the inverse transform
sampling method described above, from the truncated power-law distribution associated with each of the two states.

We performed computer simulations with this model, exploring three different cases. In the first case, we kept the walking time constant and let the rest time follow a power law but we couldn't reproduce the observed behavior in the step length distribution nor in the MSD (see Figures \ref{}Supplemental Material ). 

Finally we propose a model in which both time distributions follow a power law, $f_S(t) \propto t^{-\alpha_S}$ for rest and
$f_W(t) \propto t^{-\alpha_W}$ for walking, with $\alpha_S, \alpha_W > 0$. To explore the parameter space, we performed a sweep over
$\alpha_S \in [0.5, 2.0]$, $\alpha_W \in [1.0, 3.0]$ and $\sigma_\theta \in [0.2, 2.0]$, computing in each case the 15-minute displacement
distribution, $P(d) \propto d^{-\alpha_d}$. As an example, Figure \ref{fig:Exponents} shows these distributions for different values of
$\alpha_S$, with $\alpha_W = 1.75$ and $\sigma_\theta = 0.2$ held fixed. Across the whole sweep, the decreasing power-law regime
($\alpha_d > 0$) appears only when the rest distribution decays more slowly than the walking distribution, that is, when
$\alpha_S < \alpha_W$.


Within the region $\alpha_S < \alpha_W$, we searched for combinations that reproduce the empirically observed behavior. Figure
\ref{fig:DESPL_PL} shows the best configuration obtained, with $\sigma_\theta = 0.25$, $\alpha_W = 1.8$ and $\alpha_S = 1.1$,
that reproduces the empirical exponent, $\alpha_d \approx 2$, between $d \in [20, 200] \text{ (u.a.)}$.



As in the previous case, once the displacement distribution had been reproduced, we analyzed the MSD of this model, shown in Figure
\ref{fig:MSD_PL}. The MSD displays ballistic behaviour at short time scales, with an exponent that transitions to $\beta=1$ at long time scales.

\section*{Statistical interpretation}
Another approach consists of defining an instantaneous transition rate $\gamma(t)$, a function of the time already spent in the current state, and evaluating it at every
discrete time step $\delta t$ of the simulation to decide whether the individual switches state. Although this approach naturally
accommodates a time-dependent rate, it requires evaluating a transition probability at every time step of each bout, which considerably
increases the computational cost when simulating a large number of individuals.




This behaviour can also be explained by considering the transition probability from walking to resting, \(\gamma(t)\).  The transition probability is the probability that the tortoise will stop walking at a time between \(t\) and \(t+dt\), given that it is walking at time \(t\), i.e.
$$ \gamma(t) = \frac{P_b(t)}{\text{P. of walking at time $t$}} = \frac{P_b(t)}{1 - \text{P. of having stopped between $0$ and $t$}} = \frac{P_b(t)}{1-\int_0^t\!\!P_b(t')\,dt´} . $$
Since \(\int_1^t (\alpha-1) t^{-\alpha} = 1 - t^{1-\alpha}\), we obtain
$$ \gamma(t) = \frac{\alpha-1}{t},  t\ge1, $$
i.e.$\backslash$ the probability that the tortoise will decide to rest gets smaller the longer it has been walking.

\section*{Transition rate on a fixed maximum behaviour time}
It has been shown that tortoises if can be clasified into two groups, in which both follows a power law distributions, $f(t)$, of exponent $\alpha$ with a maximum time of behaviour, $t_{max}$, 
\cite{callenbach2025}. In order to facilitate the theoretical analysis, we can infer a transition rate from one state to another, which is a function of the time 
spent in the current state, $t$. Another thing to consider is that in our simulation we have discrete time steps, $\delta t$. We will obtain the transition rate,
through the following relation:
\begin{equation}
    \gamma (t) = 1 - P(f(t+\delta t)|f(t))
\end{equation}

Where $P(f(t+\delta t)|f(t))$ is the conditional probability of being in the same state at time $t+\delta t$ given that it was in that state at time $t$. 
This conditional probability can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    P(f(t+\delta t)|f(t)) = P(f(t)|f(t+\delta t))\frac{P(f(t+\delta t))}{P(f(t))}
\end{equation}

in which $P(f(t)|f(t+\delta t))=1$ and $f(t)=A t^{-\alpha}$ with $A$ a normalization constant. $P(f(t))$ can be calculates as follows:

\begin{equation}
    P(f(t))=\int_{t}^{t_{\text{max}}}f(t')\,dt'=\frac{A*\left(t_{\text{max}}^{1-\alpha}-t^{1-\alpha}\right)}{1-\alpha}
\end{equation}

in this equation we are assuming that the state began at time $t=0$ so if we apply this to the conditional probability we have:

\begin{equation}
    P(f(t+\delta t)|f(t)) = \frac{t_{\text{max}}^{1-\alpha}-(t+\delta t)^{1-\alpha}}{t_{\text{max}}^{1-\alpha}-t^{1-\alpha}} 
\end{equation}

Finally, we can express the transition rate as:
\begin{equation}
    \gamma (t)=\frac{(t+\delta t)^{1-\alpha}-t^{1-\alpha}}{t_{\text{max}}^{1-\alpha}-t^{1-\alpha}} 
\end{equation}

In this figure, we observe that the displacement distributions exhibit characteristic distances that are approximately multiples of
$L\,T_{\text{walk}}$. Because the model adds Gaussian noise to the direction of motion, these characteristic values lie slightly below the
aforementioned multiples, since the exact value is only attained when the individual moves in a straight line. These peaks correspond to a
given number of walking bouts completed within the 15-minute window; for example, the first peak corresponds to a single walking bout, after
which the individual remained at rest for the remainder of that window. As a consequence, these characteristic distances become blurred as
the rest-time exponent $\alpha_S$ increases, since long rest times become less likely.

Given the discrepancy between the distributions obtained and the empirical distribution, we conclude that this model cannot reproduce the
behavior observed in the tortoises, and we therefore discard it.
